# Simplicity means safety

* A top principle: safety comes from clarity and simplicity. Our core promise is that stored values cannot be recovered without the device, and are exceedingly difficult to recover even with it. Every line of code past that baseline threatens the core promise, because it makes the system harder to reason about.
* We build simple primitives with strong contracts that fail loudly. Mitigation (recovery, retry, reconciliation) is left to higher-level constructs that we or the customer apply.
* Our library offers a clear and well-enforced contract. Our priorities (safety, empathy, clarity) and the unusual constraints of our target use cases determine that contract. Each of these is preferable to adding complexity:
  - Not solving the problem. We offer no ordering, priority or retry when several read or modify operations overlap. In typical use they will likely run one after another. Under moderate load, likely at most one modify succeeds, with no promise about which one, or about the order of reads and modifies. Under plausible load, they might all succeed, or one, or none.
  - Documenting it as nonuniformly uniform. A `lock()` call might let some operations complete, and others fail or partially fail. Each case resolves predictably: in a given scenario it completes every time, or it fails every time, or it partially fails, as documented, every time. The system stays consistent, in each part and as a whole.
  - Refusing to proceed at all until the client handles the issue, where the only built-in remedy is to delete all vaults (and restore from backup).
  - Providing no middle-ground solution, if one would complicate reasonable customer use cases. For many customers, the library that best defends against a brute-force attack will be unacceptably strict, or will grow the bundle too much. Our built-in solution is to offer (a) that library as an optional plugin and (b) a prompt that lets an outside agent help the customer choose and integrate a solution.
