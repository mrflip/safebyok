
* A top principle: safety comes from clarity and simplicity. Our core promise is that stored values are irrecoverable without the device, and exceedingly difficult even with the device. Every line of code past that baseline threatens the core promise by making it difficult to reason about the system.
* We build simple primitives with strong contracts that fail loudly. Mitigation -- recovery, retry, reconciliation -- are left to higher level constructs we or the customer apply
* Our library offers a clear and well-enforced contract. That contract is determined by our priorities (safety, empathy, clarity) and the unusual constraints of our target use cases. Here are things preferable to adding complexity --
  - not solving the problem. We offer no ordering, priority or retry if multiple read/modify operations overlap. In typical operations they will likely chain. Under moderate load it's likely that at most one modify will succeed, but with no promises about which one, or sequencing among reads and modify operations. In plausible load scenarios  they might all succeed, or one, or none.
  - documenting it as nonuniformly uniform. a lock() imperative might allow some operations to complete, and others to fail or partially fail. Each individual case will resolve predictably: in that particular scenario it completes every time OR it fails every time OR it partially fails, as described, every time. The system in part and whole remain consistent.
  - refusing to proceed altogether until the client handles the issue, with the only in-built offered solution being to delete all vaults (restoring from backup).
  - providing no middle-ground solution if doing so would complicate reasonable customer use cases. For many customers, the library that best defends a brute-force attack will be unacceptably strict or bundle-size-increasing. Our builtin solution is to offer (a) that library as an optional plugin and (b) a prompt that will let an outside agent help the customer to choose and integrate a solution.
